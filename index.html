A privacy layer
for AI.

Point your AI tool at Osanwë instead of straight at the provider. Your prompt still reaches Claude or GPT and still gets answered — it just doesn’t arrive with your IP address attached, and nobody along the way can read it.

the problem

Every AI request you send
carries five different ways
to identify you.

A VPN or a Tor-style relay fixes exactly one of them. That is the gap Osanwë is built around — and being honest about which channels stay open is the point, not a disclaimer.

how it works

Three steps, and your
existing tools don’t change.

  1. 01

    Point your tool at Osanwë

    The client speaks the same API your tools already use, so you change a base URL and nothing else. Your editor, your scripts, your agent framework all keep working.

  2. 02

    Your request travels through a relay

    The relay is run by someone you don’t have to trust, because it carries an encrypted tunnel it cannot open. It sees that a request happened and where it came from — never what the request said.

  3. 03

    The provider answers normally

    Claude or GPT receives the request and responds exactly as it would have. The one thing it no longer receives is your IP address and the location it implies.

Because the encryption runs end to end between your machine and the provider, adding a hop does not add a party who can read your prompts. That is the opposite of how a normal proxy works, and it is the reason relay operators here carry far less risk than Tor exit operators.

architecture

Three parties. None of them
sees both halves.

Identity and content are split across separate operators so that no single one holds enough to identify you. This is the same shape Apple’s Private Relay uses. It rests on those operators not colluding — which we state plainly here rather than bury, because a privacy claim whose assumptions are hidden is a lie with extra steps.

The mint

knows who paid

Sells you access and never sees a single prompt. When tokens are later spent, it cannot tell which purchase they came from.

The relay

sees your address, not your words

Forwards an encrypted tunnel it has no key for. It is the only party that learns where you are, and it learns nothing else.

The gateway

sees your words, not you

Talks to the provider on your behalf. It runs inside attested secure hardware, so you can verify exactly which code will handle your prompt before you send it.

what you get, and what you don’t

The limits are the
most useful thing here.

What it does

  • Hides your IP address and location from the model provider
  • Keeps every relay operator from reading a single prompt
  • Stores no logs of your prompts or completions, anywhere
  • Works with the tools you already use, by changing one URL

What it won’t do

  • Hide you from your own writing style — no network can fix that
  • Hide which account is paying, until the mint ships in Phase 3
  • Beat an adversary who can watch the entire internet at once
  • Survive the relay and the gateway secretly colluding
  • Get you around a model’s safety policy. That is deliberate

Osanwë provides anonymity from identity linkage. It does not provide freedom from model safety policy — those are different products, and only one of them is worth building.

status

Nothing is built yet.
The design is public.

This is an architecture and a threat model, published before any code exists, so the design can be argued with while it is still cheap to change. The next step is a single measurement: how much latency one relay hop actually costs. That number decides whether Osanwë is an interactive product or a batch one, and everything downstream waits on it.
